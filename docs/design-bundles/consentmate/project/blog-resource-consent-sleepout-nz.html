Do I need a resource consent for a sleepout in NZ?

UPDATED 17 APR 2026 READ 8 MIN COVERAGE 22 COUNCILS
TL;DR · Short answer001 / 009
Most small sleepouts (≤30 m², single-storey, not used as a separate dwelling) don't need a resource consent or a building consent if they meet the Building Act's "exempt building work" rules. But the exact answer depends on your council's district plan, your section's zone, your boundary setbacks, and how you'll use the sleepout. This guide walks you through the decision tree used by the 22 biggest NZ councils so you can get a first-cut answer before paying a planner $3,500+ for the same thing.

The 5-question decision tree

Run these before you spend a dollar.

Q1

Is your sleepout ≤30 m² in floor area and single-storey?

If yes, and it's not used for sleeping by anyone other than household members, AND it's more than 1 m from any boundary, AND it's not over a drainage easement — you might fall under Schedule 1 of the Building Act 2004 (no building consent needed). That doesn't automatically mean no resource consent, though — the district plan still applies.

Q2

Is your section zoned Residential, or Rural / Rural-Residential?

  • Residential zones usually allow an accessory building like a sleepout as a "permitted activity" IF it stays inside permitted limits (setbacks, height, coverage, impermeable surfaces).
  • Rural zones often treat sleepouts as "minor residential units" and have stricter rules around water supply, wastewater, and minimum lot sizes.
  • Business / Industrial / Open Space zones usually need consent by default.

Check your council's GIS viewer (e.g. Auckland Council GeoMaps, New Plymouth District Council's MapOnline) for your section's zone.

Q3

Will the sleepout breach any of these common district-plan rules?

  • Boundary setback: typically 1.0–1.5 m from side and rear boundaries, 3.0–4.5 m from the front (road). Closer than this and you'll likely need consent.
  • Height to boundary (recession plane): imagine a 45° line rising from 2.5 m above each side boundary — your sleepout can't poke above it.
  • Building coverage: total footprint of all buildings can't exceed a percentage of the site (usually 35–50% in residential zones).
  • Impermeable surfaces: concrete/asphalt/roofs combined often can't exceed 60%.
  • Height limit: usually 8 m in residential zones. A single-storey sleepout is well under.

If any ONE of these is breached, you likely need resource consent. If all pass, you likely don't.

Q4

Will it be used as a separate dwelling (granny flat, rental, Airbnb)?

If yes, you're now applying for a minor residential unit, not a sleepout. Most councils need resource consent for this regardless of size, plus wastewater / three-waters assessment. Budget: $3,000–$8,000+ in fees and consultants.

Q5

Any of these special overlays on your section?

  • Coastal environment / coastal hazard area
  • Flood / overland flow path / ponding area
  • Significant natural area (SNA) / ecological overlay
  • Heritage / character / archaeological overlay
  • Wāhi tapu / sites of significance to mana whenua
  • Outstanding natural landscape or feature
  • Designated land (e.g. road widening, Transpower lines)

If your section has any of these (shown on council GIS maps), consent requirements tighten fast. Expect a "restricted discretionary" or "discretionary" consent status minimum.

How to get the actual answer in 10 minutes (free)

  1. Find your property's zone. Go to your council's GIS map. Type in your address. Click your property. Note the zone (e.g. "Residential – Single House Zone").
  2. Find your council's district plan. Search "[Council name] district plan" → find the chapter for your zone.
  3. Check the "permitted activity" rules in that chapter for accessory buildings. Setbacks, height to boundary, coverage, etc.
  4. Measure your proposed sleepout's distance to boundary and check against the permitted setback.
  5. Check for overlays on the GIS map — toggle on coastal, flood, and heritage layers.

Pass all permitted rules → no resource consent needed. Break one → consent needed.

Common mistakes that cost homeowners thousands

  1. Starting construction without checking — council can issue a stop-work and require retrospective consent (more expensive than doing it properly).
  2. Confusing building consent with resource consent — doing the building one but missing the resource one leaves you legally exposed.
  3. Assuming "it's only small" means exempt — Building Act exemptions are specific and unforgiving. 30 m² is the ceiling, not a soft limit.
  4. Not checking overlays — a 2 m coastal setback breach can turn a permitted activity into a notified resource consent (hearings, six-figure cost).
  5. Ringing three planners for three quotes — get a free first-cut first, ring a planner only if genuinely complex.

FAQs

How long does a resource consent take?
20 working days for non-notified residential consents (statutory timeframe). If council requests more info, the clock pauses. Add 2–6 weeks prep before submission. Total: ~2–3 months start to finish is common.
Can I appeal a declined consent?
Yes, to the Environment Court. Expensive (lawyer + planner + $5K+ filing fees). About 95% of homeowner-grade resource consent applications are approved — most declines happen at the pre-application stage.
What if my neighbour objects?
For non-notified consents, neighbour objections don't automatically block approval. Getting written approvals from affected neighbours before lodging drops the risk to near-zero.
